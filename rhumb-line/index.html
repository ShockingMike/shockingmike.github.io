Prologue · Day 1

Six empty slots, one season

  • 15 March · 05:40 · Sea smoke
  • Wind NW 12 kn · Glass 1024, steady
  • 06:10 · Lines off · Six slots empty

Every season the roaster leaves Merrowick with a wooden sample chest and six empty slots. Green coffee can be ordered from a list, but a list cannot tell you how a farm smells after rain. So we go, holding one bearing at a time, and come home when every slot is full. Pull up a stool.

Orders: six slots, six farms, one season. Cup everything. Bring home only what we would drink ourselves.

Ship’s log

Made fast at Santo Tomás at dusk. Two days by road, west and up into the Cuchumatanes. At Finca Viento Seco the last cherries were coming off the top rows. The owner wet a finger and held it up: the warm wind from Mexico, she said, is why frost never reaches them.

Ship’s log

Anchored off Cartagena in a fresh trade wind. Three days south by road, the last hour in a painted chiva with sacks on the roof. Huila was starting its mitaca, the smaller second picking. El Rumbo means “the course”. We took it as a sign and cupped until dark.

Ship’s log

Alongside at Santos. The red hills of Mogiana take their name from the old railway that once carried their coffee on its way to this quay. Picking began the morning we reached Fazenda Pedra Clara. By noon the patio was striped with cherries, raked into rows to dry.

Ship’s log

Mombasa in the long rains. Up-country by train and road to Nyeri, where Mount Kenya showed its peak for an hour at dawn. The early crop was coming in to Gakiriko Factory. At dusk the growers queued with their sacks, and each weight went into a small book.

Ship’s log

Djibouti in a hot wind. Train to Dire Dawa, on to Addis, then a day south through false banana gardens. Picking ended in January, but Bokkicho had kept one lot back in parchment. They roasted a handful in a pan over charcoal and passed it round. Jasmine.

Day 112 · 4 July

Belawan, Sumatra, Indonesia

3°47′N 98°41′E

Course from last port
Djibouti, Republic of Djibouti 11°36′N 43°08′E

Wind SW 9 kn · Squall at dawn

Ship’s log

A squall at first light, then Belawan steaming in the sun. A day up the mountain road to Takengon and Lake Laut Tawar, where the fly crop was in. Wet-hulled beans lay on tarpaulins along the lake road, darkening to jade. Kabut Tinggi means “high mist”; by four it came down.

Found and stowed

Gayo, Sumatra Kabut Tinggi Cooperative Altitude 1,300–1,600 m Process Wet-hulled Variety Tim Tim, Ateng, Gayo 1 In the cup Clove · Pipe tobacco · Molasses Spice, Roasty, Sweet

Homeward by Suez. Six slots full, and the cabin smells of six countries at once.

The eight flavour families

  • Jasmine · Orange blossom · Black tea

    Perfumed and delicate. It shows up in high-grown washed coffees and fades fast in a dark roast.

  • Blackcurrant · Red cherry · Peach

    Juicy and ripe, from bright berries to soft stone fruit. Naturals push it towards jam.

  • Bergamot · Grapefruit · Mandarin

    Crisp acidity that wakes the cup up. It sharpens as the coffee cools.

  • Caramel · Cane sugar · Molasses

    The backbone of a good roast. Sugars brown in the drum and round off the edges.

  • Milk chocolate · Dark chocolate

    Comforting and familiar. It deepens as the roast goes darker and gets along well with milk.

  • Hazelnut · Toasted almond · Pecan

    Toasty and mellow, the kind of cup you can drink every day. Common in Brazilian naturals.

  • Clove · Cinnamon · Black pepper

    Warm and a little woody. Wet-hulled Sumatran coffees carry it, often with a hint of cedar.

  • Pipe tobacco · Malt · Toast

    Deep, toasty and a little smoky. It comes from the drum as much as the farm, so darker roasts show more.

Galley · Today’s cup

A cup for the watch

The pot on the galley stove is never empty for long. Here is what’s in it today, and how to make the same cup at home.

Gayo, Sumatra, ground medium-fine for the moka pot on the galley stove.

Brewing ashore

  1. Espresso machine

    18 g · 36 g out, water at 93 °C · Fine · 25–30 s

    Judge the shot by weight in the cup. Sour and fast: grind finer. Bitter and slow: grind coarser.

  2. Pour-over or drip

    15 g · 250 g water at 93–96 °C · Medium · about 3 min

    Rinse the paper with hot water, then bloom with 30 g of water for 30–45 s before the main pour.

  3. French press

    30 g · 500 g water at 93–96 °C · Coarse · 4 min

    At four minutes, stir the crust and skim the foam. Press gently and pour it all off at once.

  4. Moka pot

    Basket level, not tamped · Hot water below the valve · Medium-fine · 3–5 min, medium heat

    Lid up. Take it off the heat as the stream turns pale, then cool the base under a cold tap.

Treat every number here as a first bearing. Taste, change one thing at a time, and log what you did.
